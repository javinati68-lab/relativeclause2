Grammar Explanation: How Relative Clauses Work

A relative clause is a subordinate clause that modifies a noun or pronoun, giving essential (defining) or extra (non-defining) information. It connects two ideas smoothly using a relative pronoun or relative adverb:

WHO / THAT

Refers to people (subject or object).

WHOM

Refers to people as the formal object of a verb or preposition.

WHICH / THAT

Refers to things, animals, or ideas.

WHOSE

Shows possession (replaces his, her, their, its).

WHERE

Refers to a place (replaces there / in which).

WHEN

Refers to a time, day, or year.

WHY

Refers to a reason (usually follows the reason).

Sample Sentences (Affirmative, Negative & Interrogative):

Affirmative (+)

The architect who designed this museum won an international prize.

(Combines: The architect won an international prize. She designed this museum.)

Negative (–)

Students who do not submit their essays will not receive credit.

(Combines: Students will not receive credit. They do not submit their essays.)

Interrogative (?)

Do you recognize the singer whose song is playing on the radio?

(Combines: Do you recognize the singer? Her song is playing on the radio.)

Instructions: Combine each pair of sentences into one single sentence using the relative pronoun/adverb in parentheses. Pay attention to affirmative, negative, and interrogative punctuation!

The Aztecs: An Empire Built on Water, Tribute, and Alliance

The civilization commonly known as the Aztec, who called themselves the Mexica, rose from modest beginnings to dominate central Mesoamerica within barely a century. According to tradition, they migrated from a legendary homeland called Aztlán and, in 1325, settled on a marshy island in Lake Texcoco, a site that more established neighbours had disregarded as unfit for farming.

The Mexica transformed this apparent disadvantage into an asset. Borrowing techniques from neighbouring peoples, they constructed chinampas, raised gardens reclaimed from the shallows, which yielded maize, beans, and squash in remarkable quantities. Causeways and canoes connected Tenochtitlán to the mainland, and the city evolved into a thriving commercial hub whose markets astonished later European visitors. Estimates suggest that Tenochtitlán housed well over one hundred thousand inhabitants, making it one of the largest cities in the world at that time, while its temples, notably the Templo Mayor, dominated the skyline.

Political power, however, derived less from agriculture than from diplomacy and force. Around 1428, Tenochtitlán, Texcoco, and Tlacopan formed the Triple Alliance, which overthrew the dominant city of Azcapotzalco. Rather than imposing a uniform culture on conquered territories, the alliance generally permitted local rulers to remain in office provided that they delivered regular tribute in goods, textiles, and sometimes captives. This arrangement proved lucrative, yet it bred considerable resentment among subject peoples.

Religion permeated every aspect of Aztec life. The Mexica believed that the gods had sacrificed themselves to create the world, so humanity bore a perpetual debt that had to be repaid through ritual offerings, including human sacrifice. Consequently, warfare served not only political ends but also sacred ones. Nevertheless, the Aztecs also prized education, and many children, including commoners, attended formal schools.

When Hernán Cortés arrived in 1519, the empire was still expanding, yet its foundations were fragile. Cortés exploited existing grievances, forging alliances with groups such as the Tlaxcalans, who had long opposed Aztec domination. Combined with devastating smallpox epidemics and superior weaponry, these alliances enabled the Spanish to capture Tenochtitlán in August 1521. Thus, the empire’s collapse resulted not from a single cause but from the convergence of internal tensions and external pressures.
